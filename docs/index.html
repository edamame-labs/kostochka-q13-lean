Question 13 for Forests

In a 2026 survey, Kierstead, Kostochka and Xiang ask whether a single counting condition decides a coloring problem on forests, exactly as it does for ordinary equitable coloring. It does not. This page builds every definition from the ground up, then takes the counterexample apart piece by piece.

The question
Is a forest SE k-choosable exactly when every vertex sits in an independent set of size ⌊n/k⌋?
The answer
No — for every k ≥ 3, and even for trees.
The witness
Two stars on 2k³ + k vertices. 57 of them when k = 3.
Margin of failure
Exactly one vertex.

Section 1

Coloring a graph, properly

Everything here happens on forests. A graph is a set of vertices (dots) with some pairs joined by edges (lines). A tree is a connected graph with no cycles; a forest is any graph with no cycles, i.e. a disjoint union of trees. Two vertices joined by an edge are adjacent, or neighbors. The degree d(v) is the number of neighbors of v.

"No cycles" is the whole of the definition, and it is more permissive than it sounds. A component of a forest can be a big branching tree, or a bare edge, or a single vertex sitting on its own — all three are trees. The counterexample in Section 7 is a forest with exactly two components, so it is worth being comfortable with the idea now.

Figure · What counts as a forest

any number of components, any size — the only rule is no cycles
Left: one forest on 11 vertices, whose components have 5, 3, 2 and 1 vertices. The lone vertex on the right of that group is a perfectly good tree — it just has no edges. Right: the same four vertices joined into a square. Every graph on this page must avoid that; Theorem 27 in Section 3 is false without it.

Proper coloring

A proper coloring assigns a color to every vertex so that no edge has both ends the same color. The set of vertices receiving one particular color is that color's color class. A color class is always an independent set: a set of vertices with no edge inside it.

Forests are easy to color properly — two colors always suffice. Click the dots below to cycle each vertex through the three colors and back to blank. Any assignment you make is proper as long as no edge turns red.

Section 2

Equitable coloring: keep the classes level

Equitable k-coloring

A proper coloring with exactly k color classes is equitable if any two classes differ in size by at most one. Equivalently, with n vertices, every class has size ⌊n/k⌋ or ⌈n/k⌉. A graph is equitably k-colorable if such a coloring exists.

The floor and ceiling brackets are just rounding: ⌊x⌋ rounds down, ⌈x⌉ rounds up. If n = 7 and k = 3, then ⌊7/3⌋ = 2 and ⌈7/3⌉ = 3, so an equitable 3-coloring has classes of sizes 3, 2, 2 — nothing else is allowed.

This is a real constraint, and it can fail. Take the star K1,5: one center joined to five leaves, n = 6. With k = 3 every class would have to have size exactly 6/3 = 2. But the center is adjacent to everything, so its class can only be the center itself — size 1. Dead on arrival.

Figure · One leaf too many

the same star, one leaf apart, with k = 3 — the class sizes tell you everything
The centre is adjacent to every leaf, so its class is always just the centre — one vertex. That caps every other class at two. On the left the four leaves fit into two classes of two. On the right there are five leaves and only two classes to hold them, so one class reaches three, and no rearrangement helps: m ≤ 2k − 2 = 4 is the whole condition.

Push a little further and you get the general rule for stars. If the center's class has size 1, then every class must have size at most 2, so the m leaves must fit into the remaining k − 1 classes at 2 apiece: m ≤ 2k − 2. That number, 2k − 2, is the exact threshold — and in the next section it will fall out of a much more general principle.

Section 3

The independence condition, and why it is enough

The quantity αv

αv(G) is the size of the largest independent set that contains the vertex v. For a star's center, α = 1: the center is adjacent to every other vertex, so no independent set containing it can hold anything else. For a leaf of that star, α = m: all the leaves together.

Here is the link to equitable coloring, and it is the whole story of Section 2 in one line. Suppose G has an equitable k-coloring. Take any vertex v and look at its own color class. That class is an independent set, it contains v, and equitability forces it to have at least ⌊n/k⌋ vertices. So:

The independence condition

αv(G) ≥ ⌊n/k⌋ for every vertex v.

Necessary for any graph to be equitably k-colorable, by the argument just given.

Necessary conditions are cheap. What makes this one special is a theorem from 1994: on forests, it is also sufficient. Nothing else can go wrong.

Theorem (Chen–Lih; Miyata–Tokunaga–Kaneko; short proof by Chang)

Let k ≥ 3. An n-vertex forest T is equitably k-colorable if and only if αv(T) ≥ ⌊n/k⌋ for every vertex v.

This is Theorem 27 in the Kierstead–Kostochka–Xiang survey. It is the theorem Question 13 tries to extend.

Try it on stars. The condition at the center reads 1 ≥ ⌊(m+1)/k⌋, which holds exactly when m + 1 < 2k, i.e. m ≤ 2k − 2 — precisely the threshold we found by hand. Move the sliders and watch the condition make the call.

Section 4

Lists, and why they break the symmetry

List assignment, L-coloring, choosability

A k-list assignment L gives every vertex v its own private palette L(v) of exactly k colors. An L-coloring is a proper coloring f with f(v) ∈ L(v) for every v. A graph is k-choosable if it has an L-coloring for every k-list assignment.

Two things about this setup are easy to get wrong, and both matter later:

  • The lists need not overlap. Across the whole graph, an L-coloring may use far more than k distinct colors — as many as there are colors in the union of all the lists.
  • Choosability is a game against an adversary. The adversary picks L first, seeing the whole graph; you color second. Setting every list to {1,…,k} is one of the adversary's options, so k-choosable always implies k-colorable. The converse fails, and here is the smallest interesting reason it fails.

Consider K2,4: two vertices x, y on the left, four on the right, every left–right pair joined. It is 2-colorable — color the left side red and the right side blue. But it is not 2-choosable. Give x the list {1,2}, give y the disjoint list {3,4}, and give the four right-hand vertices the four mixed pairs {1,3}, {1,4}, {2,3}, {2,4} — one for each way of combining a color of x with a color of y.

Remember this picture. The counterexample in Section 7 is this trap, rebuilt inside a forest. A forest cannot contain K2,4 — those are cycles — so the trap has to be sprung indirectly. That is what the equitability constraint is for.

Section 5

Three ways to say "balanced"

Combining equitability with lists takes some care, because with lists you no longer have exactly k classes to balance. The literature settled on capping class sizes from above instead. There are three definitions in play, and it is worth pinning them down before going further.

Write n for the number of vertices, and

  • q = ⌊n/k⌋, the floor;
  • cap = ⌈n/k⌉, the ceiling — a class of exactly this size is called full, and one bigger than this is overfull;
  • n mod* k, a slightly unusual remainder: the unique r ∈ {1,…,k} with n ≡ r (mod k). It agrees with the ordinary remainder except that when k divides n it equals k, not 0.

1 · Equitable k-coloring (no lists)

Exactly k classes; sizes q or cap.

2 · Equitable L-coloring — Kostochka, Pelsmajer and West, 2003

An L-coloring with no overfull class: every color is used at most cap times. That is the only requirement. Any number of colors may appear, and classes may be tiny or empty. G is equitably k-choosable if every k-list assignment admits one.

3 · Strongly equitable (SE) L-coloring — Kierstead, Kostochka and Xiang

An L-coloring with no overfull class and at most n mod* k full classes. G is SE k-choosable if every k-list assignment admits one.

Figure · What each rule allows

n = 14, k = 4 — so q = 3, cap = 4, and n mod* k = 2
All three colourings use 14 vertices and cap every class at ⌈n/k⌉ = 4. Only the first is an equitable colouring in the ordinary sense; the other two spend five colours, which lists make possible. The middle one is what the SE clause exists to forbid — pack three classes to the cap and the leftovers are stranded in classes of one.

The extra clause in (3) stops a coloring from being "balanced" in a degenerate way — packing many classes right up to the cap and leaving the rest nearly empty. The three notions nest:

SE k-choosable  ⟹  equitably k-choosable   and   SE k-choosable ⟹ equitably k-colorable

The second implication is the one that connects back to Section 3, and it deserves a proof because it is short. Feed the adversary's assignment L(v) = {1,…,k} for every v. Write n = kq' + r with r = n mod* k, so cap = q' + 1. An SE coloring uses only colors from {1,…,k}, has at most r classes of size q' + 1 and the rest of size at most q'. That accounts for at most r(q'+1) + (k−r)q' = kq' + r = n vertices — and all n vertices are colored, so the bound is tight everywhere. Exactly r classes have size q'+1 and the other k − r have size q'. That is an equitable k-coloring.

One derived quantity does all the work later, so let's isolate it now. How many vertices can k − 1 colors carry between them, in an SE coloring? Writing n = kq + r′ with the ordinary remainder 0 ≤ r′ ≤ k − 1: if r′ = 0, no class exceeds q, giving (k−1)q; if r′ ≥ 1, classes may reach q+1 but at most r′ of them do, giving (k−1)q + r′. Both cases collapse to the same expression:

Capacity of k − 1 colors

In an SE L-coloring, any k − 1 colors together hold at most n − q vertices, where q = ⌊n/k⌋.

Everything in Section 7 is a fight over that number. Use the sliders below to get a feel for how the three rules differ, and for when the SE clause is doing real work.

Section 6

Question 13, stated precisely

Theorem 27 does something genuinely useful. It takes a hard question — can this forest be equitably k-colored?, which on the face of it means searching through colorings — and replaces it with an easy one: does every vertex sit in an independent set of size ⌊n/k⌋? The second question you can simply compute from the graph. When one condition can stand in for another like this, in both directions, we say it characterizes it.

By now we have three notions of "colorable in a balanced way", each harder to satisfy than the last, plus one cheap test. Question 13 asks whether that cheap test also decides the hardest of the three.

Figure · How the four conditions relate

solid arrows always hold — the dashed one is the question
An arrow means "whenever the box at the tail holds, the box at the head holds too". The two arrows out of the top box were proved in Section 5. The double arrow on the right is Theorem 27: for forests, the cheap test and equitable colorability are the same thing. Question 13 asks for the long dashed arrow — the cheap test carrying you all the way to the top.

Question 13 · Kierstead, Kostochka and Xiang, arXiv:2504.14711

Is it true that for each k ≥ 3, an n-vertex forest T is SE k-choosable if and only if αv(T) ≥ ⌊n/k⌋ for every vertex v?

An "if and only if" is two separate claims

It pays to pull them apart, because one is easy and one is the whole problem.

"Only if" — SE k-choosable ⟹ the condition. Free. Follow the arrows down the right-hand side of the diagram. Suppose T is SE k-choosable. Hand it the list assignment where every vertex gets the same list {1,…,k} — the adversary is allowed to do that. By the computation in Section 5, an SE coloring for those lists is exactly an equitable k-coloring, so T has one. And by the argument in Section 3, each vertex's own color class is an independent set containing it of size at least ⌊n/k⌋. Done, in three steps, all of which we have already made.

"If" — the condition ⟹ SE k-choosable. The entire question. This is the dashed arrow. It has to survive every list assignment the adversary can invent, and unlike the "only if" direction there is no obvious place to start.

Why it was a reasonable thing to hope for

This is not a naive guess, and it is worth seeing why before watching it fail.

  • Forests are as simple as interesting graphs get. No cycles means no constraint ever loops back on itself, which is exactly why greedy arguments tend to work on them.
  • Without lists there is only one obstruction, and we know it. That is the content of Theorem 27. Nothing else can go wrong, so it is natural to guess nothing else goes wrong with lists either.
  • For low-degree forests, lists provably cause no trouble. Kostochka, Pelsmajer and West proved that every forest is equitably k-choosable — the KPW notion — as soon as k ≥ 1 + Δ/2, that is Δ ≤ 2k − 2. No list assignment can defeat those forests at all.
  • And the obstruction of Section 9 cannot even fire below degree k². So a counterexample has to hide out at high degree.

Figure · Where lists can start causing trouble

drawn for k = 3, against the maximum degree Δ of the forest
The left band is a theorem: below it, no list assignment defeats a forest in the KPW sense. The right band is only a possibility — high degree is necessary for the mechanism of Section 9, not sufficient. The counterexample lives far off the right of this picture: its vertex u₀ has degree 37, against k² = 9. That is why it needs 57 vertices rather than a handful, and why no small search was ever going to find it.

So the question is a fair one, and the failure is not a failure of the general idea — it is a failure at high degree, by a single vertex. That is what Section 7 builds.

Section 7

The counterexample

Fix k. Put q = 2k² + 1 and n = kq = 2k³ + k. (Section 8 explains where those numbers come from; for now take them as given.) The forest Fk is a disjoint union of two stars:

  • a star with center u₀ and leaf set A, where |A| = (k−1)q − 1;
  • a star with center w₀ and leaf set B, where |B| = 2k².

The count works out: 2 + ((k−1)q − 1) + 2k² = (k−1)q + q = kq = n, using 2k² = q − 1. Since k divides n, we have q = ⌊n/k⌋ = ⌈n/k⌉, so an SE coloring here is exactly a coloring in which no color is used more than q times. (The "at most n mod* k = k full classes" clause is automatic: k+1 classes of size q would need (k+1)q > n vertices.) In particular the counterexample defeats the weaker Kostochka–Pelsmajer–West notion too.

Index the leaves of w₀ by triples bc,d,t with c ∈ {1,…,k}, d ∈ {k+1,…,2k} and t ∈ {1,2} — that is k · k · 2 = 2k² leaves, two for each pair (c,d). Compare that with the four mixed-pair vertices of K2,4 in Section 4: same idea, doubled.

At k = 2 the whole forest fits on a screen: q = 9, n = 18, eight leaves on each star. Question 13 is about k ≥ 3, so this is not itself the answer — but it is the same object with every list written out, which is worth seeing once before the general argument.

Figure · F₂ in full

18 vertices, 16 edges, every list spelled out — no coloring applied yet
Read the four boxes across: their lists are {2,3}, {2,4}, {1,3}, {1,4} — exactly the four mixed pairs from the K2,4 trap in Section 4, one per pair (c,d), and doubled. Set f(u₀) = 1: all eight leaves of A must take color 2, which holds at most q = 9 vertices, leaving one seat. Set f(w₀) = 3: both leaves in the first box have list {2,3} and cannot take 3, so both want that one seat. 10 > 9.

Now the four things to check

  1. The independence condition holds — with zero room to spare

    The two stars are in different components, so u₀ is not adjacent to anything in w₀'s star. Hence {u₀} ∪ B is an independent set containing u₀, giving αu₀ = 1 + 2k² = 2k² + 1 = q. Exactly q — no slack. Symmetrically αw₀ = 1 + |A| = (k−1)q ≥ q. Every leaf lies in the independent set A ∪ B of size n − 2. So αv ≥ q = ⌊n/k⌋ for every vertex. ✓

  2. And it is genuinely equitably k-colorable

    Not just "passes the test" — here is the coloring. Class 1 is {u₀} ∪ B, which is independent and has exactly q vertices. The remaining {w₀} ∪ A is also independent, of size (k−1)q; chop it into k − 1 classes of size q however you like. That is k classes of size q each. ✓

  3. The adversary's lists

    Two disjoint palettes: [k] = {1,…,k} and D = {k+1,…,2k}.

    • L(u₀) = [k], and L(a) = [k] for every leaf a ∈ A;
    • L(w₀) = D — disjoint from u₀'s palette;
    • L(bc,d,t) = ([k] ∖ {c}) ∪ {d}, which has (k−1) + 1 = k colors, as required.

    Read the third line slowly. The leaf bc,d,t is the one that is armed against the pair (c,d): its only escape from u₀'s palette is the single color d.

  4. No SE L-coloring exists — a pigeonhole count

    Suppose f is an L-coloring using no color more than q times. Let c = f(u₀) ∈ [k] and d = f(w₀) ∈ D. Consider the set

    S = A ∪ {bc,d,1, bc,d,2},    |S| = (k−1)q − 1 + 2 = (k−1)q + 1.

    Every a ∈ A is adjacent to u₀ and has list [k], so f(a) ∈ [k] ∖ {c}. Each of bc,d,1, bc,d,2 is adjacent to w₀, so it cannot take d — and d was its only color outside [k] ∖ {c}. So all (k−1)q + 1 vertices of S are colored from the k − 1 colors of [k] ∖ {c}, whose total capacity is n − q = (k−1)q. One vertex too many. Contradiction. ∎

From forest to tree. Pick one leaf a₀ ∈ A and one leaf b₀ ∈ B and add the edge a₀b₀. The result Tk is a tree. Adding an edge only removes colorings, so the argument in step 4 still applies. And the independence condition survives: {u₀} ∪ B and {w₀} ∪ A are still independent (neither contains both a₀ and b₀), and the explicit equitable coloring of step 2 already puts a₀ and b₀ in different classes. So Question 13 fails for trees too.

Section 8

Why q = 2k² + 1, and not less

The construction has one free parameter and it is pinned down by two competing pressures. Meet the quantity that mediates them.

Slack

S = (k−1)q − |A|: the number of spare seats left in the k − 1 colors of [k] ∖ {c} after all of u₀'s leaves have been seated.

Pressure one: the independence condition wants S small. The trap works only if the leaves of u₀ nearly fill the k − 1 colors. The extreme case S = 0 — |A| = (k−1)q, the colors completely full — would make the trap trivial: any single extra vertex forced into [k] ∖ {c} overflows. But then |B| = n − 2 − |A| = q − 2, so

αu₀ = 1 + |B| = q − 1 < q,

and the forest fails the independence condition. It is no longer a counterexample to anything — it is just a forest that isn't equitably k-colorable, exactly as Theorem 27 predicts. This is the moment where Theorem 27 does its job. The list version escapes by taking S = 1: one seat spare, which is the smallest slack the independence condition permits.

Pressure two: the adversary needs S + 1 stuck leaves per pair. With S spare seats, one extra vertex forced into [k] ∖ {c} is not enough — you need S + 1 of them. And the adversary has to be ready for every one of the k · k = k² pairs (c,d), since you choose c and d after seeing the lists. So |B| ≥ k²(S + 1).

Now solve. From n = 2 + |A| + |B| = kq and the definition of S we get |B| = q − 2 + S, so the requirement |B| ≥ k²(S+1) becomes

q ≥ k²(S+1) + 2 − S = S(k² − 1) + k² + 2.

That is increasing in S, so the smallest legal slack gives the smallest forest. Plug in S = 1: q ≥ 2k² + 1, hence n = kq ≥ 2k³ + k. And the same formula at S = k − 1 returns q = k³ − k + 3 — which is exactly the example Kaul, Mudrock and Wagstrom published in 2020, for an unrelated purpose. Slide through the values and watch it happen.

Section 9

The general obstruction, and what is still open

Step 4 of the proof used almost nothing about stars. All it needed was: a vertex u with many neighbors whose lists are [k], plus some other vertex w with enough neighbors outside u's closed neighborhood to arm every pair (c,d). Stripping the construction down to that gives a whole family of necessary conditions.

Proposition · a general obstruction

Let T be a forest on n vertices, q = ⌊n/k⌋. Let u be a vertex, let W be a set of vertices other than u, and for each w ∈ W let Yw ⊆ N(w) ∖ N[u], with the Yw pairwise disjoint. If

d(u) − |W ∩ N(u)| + Σw∈W ⌊|Yw| / k²⌋  >  n − q,

then T is not SE k-choosable.

In words: the left side counts vertices the adversary can force into [k] ∖ {c} — the neighbors of u directly, plus ⌊|Yw|/k²⌋ from each blocking vertex w, since w's children have to be split k² ways to cover all pairs. The right side is the capacity of k − 1 colors from Section 5. Our counterexample is the case u = u₀, W = {w₀}, Yw₀ = B, where the left side is ((k−1)q − 1) + ⌊2k²/k²⌋ = (k−1)q + 1, one more than the capacity.

Note what happens with W = ∅: the condition reads d(u) > n − q, which the independence condition already rules out (since αu ≤ n − d(u)). So the proposition says something genuinely new only when some vertex has at least k² neighbors outside N[u] — degree k² is where the list obstructions begin.

Open

  • Is the independence condition plus this family of obstructions the right characterization? Unknown. It would be the natural repaired form of Theorem 27. The doubt: Kaul, Mudrock and Wagstrom could not characterize even a disjoint union of two stars for the weaker KPW notion, which hints the true condition may be messier.
  • Is a forest satisfying the independence condition SE k-choosable whenever Δ(T) < k²? Under that degree bound the obstruction above is vacuous, so a positive answer would say the two-star mechanism is the only one.
  • What is the smallest counterexample for k = 3? At most 57 vertices. No lower bound is known: exhaustive searches covered forests up to 12 vertices only over palettes of 4 or 5 colors, and a defeating assignment may need a bigger palette — the one above uses 2k = 6 colors.

Section 10

How the claim was checked

A pigeonhole argument three lines long is exactly the kind that hides an off-by-one, so it was checked three independent ways.

  • Machine-checked proof. The forest, the list assignment, the independence condition and the non-existence of an SE L-coloring are formalized for all k ≥ 2 in Lean 4 with Mathlib, in q13lean/Q13lean/Counterexample.lean. The top-level statement is Q13.question13_false; lake build succeeds with no sorry, and the only axioms used are propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound.
  • Exact computation. scripts/verify_counterexample.py confirms for k = 3, 4 that the independence condition holds, that the explicit equitable coloring is valid, and that no SE L-coloring exists — by enumerating the colors of the degree-≥2 vertices and solving a max-flow for the leaves under the SE capacities. It checks the forest, the tree variant, and the Kaul–Mudrock–Wagstrom forest.
  • Searches for something smaller. Exhaustive enumeration of forests satisfying the independence condition, plus a SAT-based counterexample-guided search, found nothing below the ranges in the table. These are not lower bounds, because the palette was capped.
